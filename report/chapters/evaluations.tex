\section{The Time Cuts}
\label{sec:time-cuts}

On 30 October Hani asked for cuts in the time an episode takes. Four changes were
built as switches and run one at a time and together on 24 seeds of \fdice, 192
episodes in all, on the chair's cluster (\BF{53}). The first lets the eye sense
from the pick configuration instead of returning home before every sense (the
fold of the sense into the approach to the pick); the second reuses renders
between two plans when no body has moved; the third lets a plan go on to its pick
when an observation confirmed what the plan assumed, instead of forcing a replan;
the fourth sets two arguments of the planner's search (its weight on search effort
and its initial complexity) (\BF{53}). \Cref{tab:timecuts} gives the results.

The search settings alone cut the per-seed wall time to 0.89 of the base's at the
median (planning 20.4 to 13.0\,s, successes 18 to 19). The fold made episodes
slower: 1.11 of the base's wall time at the median, slower on 12 of 20 seeds and
faster on one; its planning median is 36.3\,s against 19.4\,s. The cache
and the continuation changed nothing measurable (0.99 each). Added to the other
three cuts, the search settings brought the wall time to 0.55 of that arm's and
the successes from 21 to 14 on 20 seeds; all four together gave 0.78 of the base's
wall time and 14 successes against 18. The entry's own conclusion is that the
search settings are the time cut, that the fold costs planning time and, with the
others, successes, and that the cache and the continuation do nothing measurable
(\BF{53}). The slow episodes are replan loops of observe, replan, observe, one
with 208 planner calls and 102 observes (seed 6, cache arm) and one with 160 calls
and 78 observes (seed 19, base); the entry calls this a finding of its own that
none of the four cuts addresses and that it does not analyse (\BF{53}).

Hani's decision on 31 October: ``okay, i want only fix four applied; the other
fixes stay on some branch for reference'' (\BF{57}). The pipeline keeps the search
settings; the other three cuts sit on a reference branch. At Hani's request the
fold came back the same day as a switch of its own (\BF{57}, \BF{52}).

The entry's findings, in order (\BF{59}): more solver iterations alone made the
carry worse (0.54 lost per carry against the base's 0.15); together with a position
hold at 10\,N per finger they cured it (0 of 54 and 0 of 60 carries lost against 8
of 52 and 9 of 51). Keeping the pose uncertainty cured the cup loop (one timeout
against eight) but the die was then lost in 0.34 of the carries, which the entry
notes as not significant alone; adding the grasp yaw nearer the wrist's home cured
the hold almost alone (1 of 50) but opened a new cup loop on one scene (ten
timeouts), and the entry leaves open why the chosen set avoids it. Limiting the carry's acceleration added nothing (0 of 54 against 1
of 50) and cost execution time (a factor 1.22); the hold check gave no safer carry
(0.18 against 0.22 per carry), which the entry calls ``an honest ending, not a
fix''. The chosen set held the die in all 60 carries, with no timeout and no seed
whose three repetitions disagree; ``the prediction stated before the eval held''
(\BF{59}).

A second job on 2 November replicated the result with the base, the chosen set
and all six fixes together, three repetitions each: the chosen set again 60 of 60
with no die lost in 60 carries; the base 41 of 60 with 13 dice lost in 54 carries
(0.24); all six 60 of 60. Pooled over five jobs the base reads 209 of 300, with 53
dice lost in 262 carries (0.20, interval 0.16 to 0.26) and 37 timeouts (\BF{59}).
Our base disagrees with itself on 13 of 20 seeds over its 240 episodes (a seed's
three repetitions do not all end the same way), TAMPURA on 9 of 20, and the chosen
set on none (\BF{59}). The entry adds three limits. All numbers are from three
scenes, because TAMPURA's seeds load only three. The p values it reports are
descriptive for the same reason. And the comparison is planner against planner,
each in its own simulation: TAMPURA runs PyBullet's 50 solver iterations, ours 200
(\BF{59}). On 2 November Hani decided that the four switches of the chosen set are
on by default; ``base'' in earlier comparisons means all switches off (\BF{62}).

\section{Reading the Thesis Sweep Again}
\label{sec:timeout-reading}

While reviewing the plan of path 6, the orchestrator found that the run logger
labels every failed run without a give-up reason ``timeout'' (\BF{68}). In the
thesis sweep's stack cells with the adaptive partition, 29 rows carry that label.
Of these, 25 ended after 8 to 21 seconds, a symbolic stack success that the
end-of-run physics check rejected (the case recorded as CB\#122), and 4 reached
the 1\,800\,s budget (\BF{68}). The thesis counts all of them as cells that reached
the budget: its 24\,\% on stack (16\,\% on holding, 31\,\% on find-and-tray-stack)
is the share of rows labelled ``timeout'' across the three variants, 65 of 270
stack cells (\BF{68}). Path 6's report counts a timeout only for the runner's
stub at the cap (\BF{68}). The statement of the thesis that qualifies is listed in
\Cref{tab:qualify}.

\label{sec:planned-cluster}

Path 6 has planned, and not run, a cluster comparison of our code now (arm A, the
stable tag plus a fix of the sweep runner), the thesis commit (arm B, 6b00cd7)

the go to the numbers (\TK{6}).

The plan states four facts to know before saying yes (\TK{6}). The thesis sweep ran
13 days in the live checkout, so the commit stands for it. The benchmark has moved:
no random-pairs layout of 104 buildable pairs is identical to the thesis's, and 50
of 141 stack layouts are, so arm A against arm B is unpaired and measures code and
benchmark together. The goal holding was scored by two different physical tests,
the weld and the pads at 0.5\,N. And the four switches of \BF{62} were never run on
our tabletop scenes. Hani answered yes to phase 1 on 7 November at 11:08, before
rule 12 of the tracks required Hani's written approval of every deviation. All
three deviations are answered: our physical world-state test is the standard
success test for both arms, with TAMPURA's reward beside it; arm A is held to
TAMPURA's 20-action horizon by a cut-off applied to each episode's own action
count, with the numbers with and without the cap recounted from the same records;
and the run conditions of arm B on the cluster were accepted with checks on 7
November about 14:20. Hani's yes to the cluster run of 11:08 stands unless the
node-hours change; nothing has been pushed or run on the cluster, and the run waits
for Hani's visual check under rule 15 (\TK{6}).

\section{Published Numbers and Our Check, by Task}
\label{sec:published}

Each table below sets what the paper for a task publishes beside what our runs of
the same approach, or of ours, measured, and states whether our run reproduces the
published number. The published values were read from the papers in the course
of writing this report; the citation record (\ID{citations}{citation record}) gives
for each value the paper, the table, the setting the paper states and the date it
was read. Success counts are ours; the TAMPURA paper reports a discounted
return with a discount of 0.98 per step. The paper states the goal as a set of
belief states and not the reward value; read with a reward of 1 on reaching the
goal, a success at step $t$ counts $0.98^t$, so a return of $r$ means a success
rate of at least $r$.

\paragraph{Simple walkers on LW1's states.} Path 10's first step measured how
often policies that do no reasoning win LW1's own 100 hidden states
(\Cref{tab:walkers}). A straight line along one axis, a walker that goes to the
nearest unvisited cell and never refuses a move into an unknown cell (the optimistic
frontier walker), and a random walk are the policies; the rule-based explorer E of
path 4 is the reference that does reason: a visited cell without a breeze makes its
neighbours pit-free, without a stench monster-free, and it moves only over visited
or provably safe cells (\TK{10}).

The track's reading is that the suspicion that a straight line solves every
instance is false by a wide margin, and that the test has a luck floor of a third of the
states at 5x5 and a fifth at 7x7 and 10x10: a walker that never refuses an unknown
cell and never looks at a percept still wins 34 of 100 at 5x5 (\TK{10}). It also found no percept at the
start and no hazard in a start neighbour in any of the 1\,000 instances measured,
and a hazard-free path to the gold in each of them, with median length 4, 6, 9 and
13 moves at 5x5, 7x7, 10x10 and 15x15 (\TK{10}). At 20x20 and 25x25 the one-step
rule alone stops in 36 and 57 of the 100 states (\TK{10}).

\paragraph{The check against the published numbers.} \emph{For the simple walkers
no published number exists among the papers read for this report}: neither the LW1,
CLG, K-replanner and SDR papers nor the beam-tracking paper report a straight-line
walk, a frontier or sweep walk, a random walk or a rule-based explorer without
belief tracking on LW1's Wumpus instances, and three further searches for such a
baseline found none (\ID{citations}{citation record}). The luck floor of
\Cref{tab:walkers} is therefore our own measurement, not a check against a
published one. The explorer E is the nearest published analogue of the beam-tracking
policy: both reason about safe cells. E wins all 100 states up
to 15x15 where the published policy wins 93.6 to 98.3\,\% of its 1\,000 runs, and 64
and 43 of 100 at 20x20 and 25x25 where the published policy wins 97.8 and
94.0\,\% \cite{bonet2013causal}. The two sets of instances differ (LW1's 100 hidden
states per size against the paper's 1\,000 runs, of which some cannot be solved
from the information the policy gathers), so the comparison shows only that the
belief-tracking policy is far ahead at the larger sizes and that a one-step rule
suffices where the instances have a hazard-free route. No Wumpus run of ours with
the Semantic Boxels planner has a result yet (\Cref{ch:progress}).
